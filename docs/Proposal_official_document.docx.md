***Executive Summary***  
This project proposal addresses both 1\) the importance of understanding passenger traffic flow at top metropolitan U.S. airports in relation to customer experience and government staffing, and 2\) prior machine learning methods used to forecast airport passenger flows. The paper explains the in-depth relevance of the business problem, applicable usages of optimizing existing solutions, and various raw datasets published by the U.S. government related to this topic.

***Business Problem***  
	While airports across the U.S. impressively service millions of passengers per day, over 61% of American air travelers cite “long lines at airport security” as one of the “top three inconveniencies" encountered by passengers (International Airport Review, 2024). Depending on the average and variance in passenger check-in times between different airports, this can impact users’ long-term preferences on which airport to fly through and overall customer satisfaction in the airline experience.   
	A recent 2026 article summarizing TSA delays, including “which airports have long\[est\] lines, and how to check the wait times” and recent government shutdowns impacting 60,000+  TSA employees, demonstrates the ongoing customer interest in understanding associated airport efficiency for their own travel plans (Business Insider, 2026).   
Given raw datasets of airline flight schedules, historical traffic flow of each airport published by government sources, on-time performance data, as well as holidays, we want to help both the U.S. government staffing agencies and airports better understand their overall passenger traffic flow patterns. Implementing our own improved machine learning model to predict passenger traffic flow across U.S. airports throughout different seasons will enable airports to better understand their awareness of forecasted revenue, ongoing traffic operations, and customer experience. Developing a better understanding of fluctuations of passenger flow for the airports in the linked existing government reports can enable both the TSA government organization and individual airports to staff sufficient employees to save time for passengers, especially given the previously mentioned news articles on travel frustration and long check-in lines.

***Historical Examples***  
Several prior studies have already applied machine learning to forecast airport passenger flows and checkpoint demand. We have highlighted two below, which give us a baseline to further develop our own project. 

***Paris-Charles de Gaulle (CDG) Example***  
*Monmousseau et al. (2020)* predict 10‑minute passenger counts at eight security checkpoints in Paris Charles de Gaulle Airport using Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) neural networks and random forests trained on detailed flight schedules, expected passenger loads, and calendar features (holidays, day of week, weekends, etc.). They report that their LSTM models consistently outperform the airport’s existing forecasting tool across all checkpoints on theoretical metrics such as R2, mean absolute error, and daily Pearson correlation, and also reduce estimated agent‑hours for most checkpoints when evaluated with staffing and wait‑time–based operational metrics.

***London-Heathrow (LHR) Example***  
	*Guo, Grushka‑Cockayne, and De Reyck (2021)* built a predictive system at London-Heathrow. Their approach first fits a regression tree on 3.7 million passenger records to identify 16 behaviorally distinct segments (e.g., by terminal, region, cabin class, perceived connection time, and load factor), then fits distributions within each leaf and uses simulations to generate 15‑minute quantile forecasts of arrivals at immigration and security. Their model delivers more accurate point and interval forecasts, is fast enough for updates every 15 minutes, and is currently used in Heathrow’s operations center to identify at‑risk connecting passengers and to inform real‑time resourcing decisions for immigration desks and security lanes.

***Datasets***  
This section describes the datasets our team will use to build the POC ML system. Together, these sources provide both the prediction target (hourly passenger throughput) and the features (flight schedules, passenger volumes, and calendar indicators) needed to forecast TSA checkpoint demand.

**TSA Checkpoint Throughput Data**  
The Transportation Security Administration publishes weekly PDF reports containing hourly passenger throughput data for each airport and checkpoint through its FOIA Electronic Reading Room (Transportation Security Administration, 2026). Each report contains the following fields: Date, Hour of Day, Airport, City, State, Checkpoint, and Total Passengers (including Known Crewmember program participants). The data covers 37 airports and 62 individual checkpoints, with hourly granularity. These reports have been published weekly since late 2017\. Because the raw data is distributed across hundreds of individual PDF files, our team will use a pre-processed version maintained by Lor (2025), available at https://github.com/mikelor/TsaThroughput. This repository provides clean CSV files compiled from the original TSA PDFs, spanning December 30, 2018, through May 31, 2025\. This dataset serves as the prediction target for our model: given a specific airport, checkpoint, date, and hour, we aim to predict passenger throughput volume.

**BTS Reporting Carrier On-Time Performance Data**  
The Bureau of Transportation Statistics publishes detailed flight-level data through its Reporting Carrier On-Time Performance database (Bureau of Transportation Statistics, 2026), with data available from 1987 to November 2025\. The data is downloadable on a per-month basis. Key fields for our project include FlightDate, Origin and Destination airport codes, CRSDepTime (scheduled departure time), Reporting\_Airline, and indicators for cancellations and diversions. This dataset allows us to construct hourly flight departure counts per airport, which serve as the primary demand driver for checkpoint arrivals, since passengers typically arrive at the airport one to two hours before their scheduled departure.

**BTS T-100 Domestic Segment Data**  
The T-100 Domestic Segment table from the Bureau of Transportation Statistics provides monthly aggregate data on passenger volumes, seat capacity, scheduled departures, and departures performed for all domestic non-stop segments operated by U.S. and foreign carriers, with data available through December 2025\. Key fields include Passengers (transported per segment), Seats (available capacity), DepScheduled, DepPerformed, and aircraft type, reported by origin and destination airport. This dataset enables us to estimate average load factors per route, which helps translate scheduled flights into expected passenger counts at each airport.

**U.S. Federal Holiday Data**  
For calendar-based features, we will use the Python holidays library to programmatically generate U.S. federal holiday indicators across the full date range of our training data. The official source for federal holiday designations is the U.S. Office of Personnel Management (2026). Additional calendar features, such as day of week, month, and proximity to major holiday weekends, will be engineered from the date field.
